Interactive Cell Visualization

By Emilia Szynwald

Multilayer quantum devices, such as twisted bilayers, are cutting-edge platforms for exploring exotic electronic states like Wigner crystals and excitonic phases. This tool helps visualize the atomic lattice structures that form these remarkable materials.

Key Applications: By adjusting cell parameters, you can explore how electron configurations optimize within different lattice geometries. This is crucial for studying systems with defects (vacancies and interstitials) and understanding electron-hole interactions in bilayer systems. Our computational approach leverages modern machine learning frameworks to make these complex calculations accessible to both experimentalists and theorists.

Choose Your Lattice Structure:

Square Lattice

Perpendicular axes • Equal spacing • 90° angles

The simplest 2D lattice with perpendicular axes of equal length. Common in oxide materials and certain transition metal dichalcogenides. Ideal for modeling commensurate systems with integer fillings.

Honeycomb Lattice

Hexagonal structure • 60° angles • Two sublattices

Characteristic of graphene and TMD monolayers like WSe₂. Features two inequivalent sublattices, enabling study of exotic phases and Moiré patterns in twisted bilayers.

Understanding Your Visualization

{% if cell_type == 'square' %} Square Lattice: This structure shows a simple cubic arrangement where atoms sit at 90° to each other. The lattice constant (a={{ lattice_constant }}) determines the spacing between neighboring atoms. This type of lattice is found in certain oxide materials and is useful for modeling electron configurations at commensurate (integer) fillings. {% else %} Honeycomb Lattice: This hexagonal structure is characteristic of materials like graphene and transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) such as WSe₂. The two sublattices allow for rich physics including Dirac cones and topological phases. When two layers are twisted, Moiré patterns emerge that enable exotic quantum states. {% endif %}

Applications in Research: These visualizations help understand how electrons optimize their positions in 2D materials, crucial for studying quantum phenomena like Wigner crystallization and superconductivity in twisted bilayers.
